#### Troubleshooting Low Fuel Pressure

- If the pressure is too high, the solenoid will not open and the generator will not start.
- If the pressure is too low, the generator will be starved for fuel and will not start or will not run smoothly or be able to support a load.

### Common Causes

In most cases, a dealer or installer will need to address these issues: [Where Can I Find a Generac Dealer in the U.S. Or Canada?](https://support.generac.com/s/article/How-Can-I-Find-a-Generac-Service-Dealer-in-My-Area)

- The liquid propane tank pressure is inadequate - [How Do I Determine the Proper Fuel Pressure for My Air-Cooled Home Standby Generator?](https://support.generac.com/s/article/How-Do-I-Determine-the-Proper-Fuel-Pressure-for-My-Air-Cooled-Home-Standby-Generator)

- The natural gas service or meter is inadequate - [How Do I Determine the Proper Fuel Pressure for My Air-Cooled Home Standby Generator?](https://support.generac.com/s/article/How-Do-I-Determine-the-Proper-Fuel-Pressure-for-My-Air-Cooled-Home-Standby-Generator)

- The pressure regulator is not set or sized correctly - This should be done by the dealer, installer, or gas company.
- The fuel line is not properly sized - [How Do I Determine the Fuel Line Sizing for My Air-Cooled Home Standby Generator?](https://support.generac.com/s/article/How-Do-I-Determine-the-Fuel-Line-Sizing-for-My-Air-Cooled-Home-Standby-Generator)

Proper fuel flow is critical for generator operation under full load. Incorrect fuel pressure, undersized lines, or improper fuel conversions are not covered under warranty.

You need a manometer. Need around 12” of propane pressure….not sure how that relates to psi. You need to see if there is any fluctuation in fuel during start and run.

You need to measure pressure on test port below fuel solenoid.

While trying to start the generator, press a very small phillips screwdriver thru one of the 5 small holes on the demand regulator face (regulator inside generator). See if unit will start.

### What to Do When Your Generac Won’t Start

It’s the moment you need it, and nothing happens. A generator that won’t start is one of the most common and frustrating issues homeowners face. More often than not, the culprit is the battery. Just like in your car, the generator’s battery can weaken over time and may not have enough power to turn the engine over. Before you panic, check the control panel for any error codes that might point you in the right direction. Other potential causes could be a lack of fuel, a tripped circuit breaker on the unit itself, or an issue with the automatic transfer switch.

### How to Handle an Overheating Generator

If your generator feels excessively hot or shuts down unexpectedly during operation, it may be overheating. This is a serious issue that the generator’s safety systems are designed to prevent, as it can cause significant engine damage. The most common cause is restricted airflow. Take a look around your generator and make sure the vents are clear of any obstructions like leaves, snow, or debris. Overheating can also be caused by low oil or coolant levels. If clearing the vents doesn’t solve the problem, it’s best to keep the unit off and call a professional for a thorough inspection.

### Fixing a Constantly Tripping Circuit Breaker

A circuit breaker is a safety device designed to trip when it detects an electrical overload. If your generator’s breaker keeps tripping, it’s likely trying to power more appliances than it can handle. Try reducing the electrical load by turning off non-essential items and see if that resolves the issue. If the breaker trips even with a minimal load, it could indicate a more serious problem, such as a short circuit in an appliance or a fault within the generator itself. Persistent tripping is a sign you should contact an expert for [electrician repairs](https://mtruhl.com/electrician-repairs/).

### Dealing with a Dead Generator Battery

A dead battery is the leading reason a Generac generator fails to start. Generator batteries typically have a lifespan of two to three years, and their ability to hold a charge diminishes over time. If your generator hasn’t run its weekly self-test cycle, the battery may have drained. You can check the battery terminals for any signs of corrosion, which looks like a white or greenish powder, and make sure the connections are tight. If the battery is old or won’t hold a charge, it will need to be replaced. Regular [generator maintenance](https://mtruhl.com/generac/generator-maintenance/) helps catch a failing battery before it becomes a problem during an outage.

### Solving Fuel Delivery Issues

A generator can’t run without a consistent fuel supply. If your unit is connected to a natural gas or propane line, the issue could be a closed valve or a problem with the utility supply itself. For generators that use diesel or gasoline, the problem might be as simple as an empty tank. However, it could also be due to old or contaminated fuel, which can clog the fuel filter and lines. Stale fuel loses its combustibility and can leave gummy deposits in the fuel system. If you suspect a fuel delivery issue, it’s often best to have a technician diagnose and fix it.

### What Low Oil Pressure Means for Your Generac

A low oil pressure warning is a critical alert that will cause your generator to shut down to prevent catastrophic engine damage. Your first step should be to turn the generator off and let it cool down completely. Then, check the oil level using the dipstick. If it’s low, add the manufacturer-recommended oil slowly, being careful not to overfill it. If the oil level is correct but the warning persists after restarting, there may be a more serious internal issue, such as a faulty oil pressure sensor or a problem with the oil pump. This requires immediate attention from a qualified [Generac technician](https://mtruhl.com/generac/generator-service-and-repair/).

### Decoding the Overcrank Error (Code 1100)

This is one of the most common error codes, and it simply means your generator tried to start several times but failed. It’s not a sign of a major failure, but rather an alert that something is preventing the engine from turning over. The first thing you should do is try to reset the generator and clear the alarm. Next, check the most obvious culprit: the fuel supply. Make sure the gas valve is in the “On” position and that there is fuel in the tank or that the line from your utility is open. If you’ve checked the fuel and the error code reappears after another start attempt, it’s time to call a professional.

### Understanding the Low Oil Pressure Error (Code 1300)

Your generator’s engine needs a steady supply of oil to run smoothly and prevent damage. The Low Oil Pressure error (Code 1300) is a critical safety alert that shuts the unit down to protect the engine from friction and overheating. When you see this code, your first step is to check the oil level using the dipstick. If it’s low, add the correct type of oil slowly, checking the level frequently to avoid overfilling. If the oil level is fine but the error persists, it could indicate a faulty sensor or a more serious internal issue. Continuing to run the generator could cause severe engine damage, so your next call should be for professional [Generac maintenance](https://mtruhl.com/generac/generator-maintenance/).

### What the Overspeed Error Means (Codes 1200/1205)

The Overspeed error code means the generator’s engine is running much faster than its designated RPM (revolutions per minute). This is a serious issue because an engine running too fast can cause significant damage to both the generator and the appliances it’s powering. The generator will automatically shut down to protect itself. This problem is often caused by issues with the fuel governor or ignition coils, which regulate engine speed. An Overspeed error is not something you should try to fix yourself. It requires specialized diagnostic tools and expertise to resolve safely, so please contact a certified technician for assistance.

### Troubleshooting the Undervoltage Error (Code 1900)

When your generator displays an Undervoltage error, it’s telling you that it isn’t producing enough voltage to safely power your home or business. The unit shuts down to protect your sensitive electronics and appliances from damage caused by an unstable power supply. The cause of an undervoltage error can be complex, ranging from a tripped circuit breaker on the generator itself to a faulty voltage regulator or a problem with the internal windings. While you can check the circuit breaker, most causes of this error require professional diagnosis. A technician can safely test the electrical components and perform the necessary [electrician repairs](https://mtruhl.com/electrician-repairs/).

### Fixing an RPM Sense Loss Error (Codes 1501-1515)

This error code indicates that the generator’s controller has lost the ability to read the engine’s speed. Without this crucial information, the generator can’t regulate its performance and will shut down as a safety measure. Sometimes, a simple restart can clear this alarm if it was a temporary glitch. You can also try reducing the electrical load on the generator, as a sudden, heavy demand can sometimes trigger this fault. However, if the error code keeps coming back, it likely points to a failing sensor or an issue with the control board. This requires professional attention to diagnose and fix correctly.

### How to Tell If the Battery Is Dead

More often than not, the reason a generator won’t start is a dead or failing battery. Just like a car battery, your generator’s battery has a finite lifespan, typically around two to three years. If you hear a clicking or chattering sound when it tries to start, the battery is the first place you should look. Check the terminals for any corrosion (a white, powdery substance) and make sure the connections are clean and tight. If the battery is a few years old or has been exposed to extreme temperatures, it may no longer be able to hold a charge. ### How to Check for Fuel Blockages

Your generator needs a steady flow of fuel to run, and any interruption can prevent it from starting. For generators that run on liquid propane or natural gas, this could mean an issue with the supply line or valve. If your generator uses gasoline, old or contaminated fuel can clog the fuel filter, lines, or carburetor. Start with the easiest check: make sure you have enough fuel. If the tank is full, the next step is to inspect the fuel filter for debris. If you suspect a more serious blockage in the fuel line or carburetor, it’s best to call for professional [Generac service and repair](https://mtruhl.com/generac/generator-service-and-repair/) to avoid causing further damage.

### How to Inspect the Ignition System

If the battery is good and fuel is flowing, the problem might lie with the ignition system. The spark plugs are a key component here, as they create the spark needed to ignite the fuel. If you hear the engine trying to turn over but it just won’t catch, a faulty spark plug could be to blame. You can perform a visual inspection to see if the plugs are dirty, oily, or worn down. While replacing spark plugs is a manageable task for some, if you’re not comfortable doing it or if new plugs don’t solve the problem, there may be a deeper issue within the ignition system that requires an expert diagnosis.

### How to Check Switches and Connections

Sometimes the solution is surprisingly simple. Before you get too deep into troubleshooting, take a moment to double-check all the essential switches and connections. Ensure the generator’s main switch is set to the “On” or “Auto” position. On some older models, you may need to give the switch an extra push to make sure it’s fully engaged. Also, check the circuit breaker on the generator itself to see if it has tripped. A quick look at the automatic transfer switch can also confirm it’s set correctly. Taking a moment to verify these basic settings can save you a lot of time and frustration.

### How to Spot Poor Fuel Quality

For gasoline-powered generators, the quality of your fuel is just as important as the quantity. Gasoline degrades over time, and using old fuel is a common cause of starting issues. Stale gas can leave gummy deposits that clog the carburetor and fuel system. To prevent this, always use fresh, high-quality fuel and add a fuel stabilizer if you don’t expect to use the generator for an extended period. This simple step is crucial for keeping portable [Generac generators](https://mtruhl.com/generac/) ready to go when you need them. #### Why Is My Generac Generator Overheating?

An overheating generator is a clear sign that something is wrong. Your Generac unit has built-in safety features that will likely shut it down to prevent serious damage, but it’s important to figure out the root cause. ### Look for Blocked Vents and Poor Airflow

Your generator needs to breathe. Just like any engine, it produces a lot of heat and relies on a steady flow of air to stay cool. If the vents are blocked, that heat gets trapped, and the engine temperature will quickly rise. Take a walk around your generator and look for anything that might be obstructing airflow. Common culprits include overgrown bushes, piled-up leaves, or snow drifts in the winter. You should always maintain a few feet of clear space on all sides of the unit. A quick cleanup is often all it takes to solve an overheating issue and keep your [Generac generator](https://mtruhl.com/generac/) running safely.

### Confirm You Have the Right Oil Level

Engine oil does more than just lubricate moving parts; it also plays a huge role in cooling the engine by drawing heat away from critical components. If the oil level is too low, the engine can’t cool itself effectively, leading to overheating and potentially severe damage. Checking the oil is simple. First, make sure the generator is turned off and has had time to cool down. Then, locate the dipstick, pull it out, wipe it clean, and fully re-insert it. Pull it out again to get an accurate reading. If the level is low, add the manufacturer-recommended oil slowly to avoid overfilling. If you see a layer of gunk on the engine, it’s time for a gentle cleaning. While you’re inspecting the unit, keep an eye out for any puddles or wet spots underneath. ### Start with the Fuel Level and Quality

One of the most common reasons a generator won’t start is a fuel issue. If your generator is connected to a natural gas or propane line, start by making sure the fuel valve is in the “On” position. It’s a simple step that’s surprisingly easy to overlook. If your generator uses a propane tank, check the gauge to ensure it isn’t empty. For portable models, old or contaminated fuel can clog the fuel lines and carburetor. It’s important to use fresh, high-quality fuel. If the gas has been sitting for months, it may need to be replaced.

### Inspect the Battery and Its Connections

Much like a car, your generator relies on a battery to start. In fact, a weak or dead battery is a leading cause of startup failure. Begin by visually inspecting the battery terminals. Are the connections tight and free of corrosion? A fuzzy, whitish-blue buildup can prevent the battery from delivering enough power. If you see any, you can carefully clean it off with a wire brush. If the connections are clean and tight but the generator still won’t start, the battery itself may be old or damaged. Most generator batteries last about two to three years. If yours is getting old, it might be time for a professional [Generac service and repair](https://mtruhl.com/generac/generator-service-and-repair/) visit.

### Examine the Air Filter for Blockages

An engine needs a steady flow of clean air to run properly. A clogged or dirty air filter can restrict airflow, causing the generator to run poorly or shut down. Locate the air filter housing, open it, and take a look at the filter. If it’s filled with dirt and debris, it’s time for a replacement. While you’re there, check the area around your generator. Make sure that vents are not blocked by leaves, snow, or other debris. Proper ventilation is crucial to prevent the unit from overheating, especially when it’s running for an extended period.

### Test the Circuit Breakers and Try a Reset

If your generator is running but your home has no power, the issue might be a tripped circuit breaker. Circuit breakers are safety devices designed to protect your generator and your home’s electrical system from overloads. First, check the circuit breaker located directly on the generator and make sure it’s in the “On” position. If it has tripped, switch it completely off before flipping it back on. Next, check the breakers in your home’s main electrical panel, including the one connected to the generator. If you frequently deal with tripped breakers, it may signal a deeper issue that requires an [electrician’s repairs](https://mtruhl.com/electrician-repairs/).

### Verify Your Oil Level and Condition

Oil is essential for protecting your generator’s engine from damage. Most Generac generators have a low-oil sensor that will automatically shut the unit down if the oil level drops too low. This is a critical safety feature, but it can be confusing if you don’t know what’s happening. To check the oil, remove the dipstick, wipe it clean, reinsert it fully, and then pull it out again to get an accurate reading. If the level is low, add the specific type of oil recommended in your owner’s manual. Regular oil checks are a key part of routine [Generac maintenance](https://mtruhl.com/generac/generator-maintenance/) and are especially important before seasons with a high risk of power outages.

### Don’t Forget to Check the Coolant

For liquid-cooled Generac models, maintaining the right coolant level is just as important as checking the oil. Coolant prevents the engine from overheating during long run times. If the coolant level gets too low, a sensor will stop the generator from starting to prevent serious engine damage. You can check the level by looking at the coolant overflow reservoir; there should be “Full” and “Low” markings on the side. If the level is low, you can top it off, but be sure to use the correct type of coolant specified by the manufacturer. Always wait for the engine to cool down completely before opening the radiator cap.

### Why You Should Run Your Generator Weekly

One of the easiest and most effective things you can do is to run your generator for about 10 to 15 minutes every week. Most Generac models do this automatically through a self-test exercise, but it’s always good to confirm it’s happening. This short run time keeps the engine parts lubricated, prevents moisture buildup, and ensures the battery stays charged. It’s like a quick warm-up that keeps everything in working order. This simple habit helps prevent the seals from drying out and ensures the electrical components are ready to go, significantly reducing the chance of failure during an actual outage.

### Stick to a Regular Oil and Filter Schedule

Just like your car, your generator’s engine needs clean oil to run smoothly. Over time, oil breaks down and collects dirt and debris, which can cause friction and wear on engine components. ### How to Test and Maintain the Battery

A dead or weak battery is one of the most common reasons a generator fails to start. Generator batteries typically need to be replaced every two to three years, but their health depends on regular attention. The weekly self-test helps keep the battery charged, but you should also visually inspect it. Look for clean, tight connections and check for any signs of corrosion on the terminals. Fuel-related problems can stop a generator in its tracks. If you have a portable generator, old or contaminated fuel can clog the fuel lines and carburetor. It’s important to use fresh, high-quality fuel and a stabilizer if it will be sitting for a while. For standby generators that run on natural gas or propane, the fuel supply is more stable. During a professional tune-up, a technician will inspect the entire fuel system to make sure everything is flowing as it should.

### When to Clean or Replace the Air Filter

The air filter has a simple but vital job: it keeps dust, dirt, and other debris from getting inside the engine. A clogged air filter restricts airflow, which forces the engine to work harder, burn more fuel, and run less efficiently. Over time, this can lead to engine damage. Checking the air filter regularly and cleaning or replacing it as needed is a quick and easy task. A clean filter ensures your generator gets the clean air it needs for optimal combustion, helping it run more smoothly and last longer.

### Inspect and Replace Spark Plugs as Needed

The spark plug provides the spark needed to ignite the fuel and start the engine. A dirty, old, or damaged spark plug can cause a range of problems, from difficulty starting to rough running and reduced efficiency. Spark plugs don’t last forever and should be inspected annually for wear and tear. Replacing them according to your Generac’s maintenance schedule is an inexpensive way to ensure reliable starts and efficient engine performance. It’s a small part that plays a huge role in your generator’s overall health.

#### Frequently Asked Questions

**What’s the first thing I should check if my generator won’t start?** Before you worry, start with the two most common culprits: the battery and the fuel. A clicking sound often points to a weak or dead battery, so check that the connections are clean and tight. If the battery seems fine, confirm your fuel supply. For natural gas or propane models, make sure the valve is open. For portable units, ensure the tank has fresh gasoline. These simple checks often solve the problem without needing a service call.

**How can I tell if a generator issue is a simple fix or if I need to call a professional?** A good rule of thumb is to handle the basics but leave the complex systems to an expert. You can safely check fluid levels, look for blocked vents, and reset a tripped circuit breaker. However, if you see a persistent error code, hear loud or unusual engine noises, or suspect an issue with the fuel line or electrical transfer switch, it’s time to call a certified technician. Working on these systems without training can be unsafe and may void your warranty.

**Is it really necessary to have my generator professionally serviced every year?** Absolutely. Think of it as an insurance policy for your investment. An annual service visit allows a technician to catch small issues before they become major failures during an outage. They will change the oil and filters, replace spark plugs, and test the battery, which are all critical for engine health. This preventative care is the single best way to ensure your generator runs reliably for years to come.

**My generator is running, but my house has no power. What should I do?** This situation usually points to a tripped circuit breaker. Start by checking the main breaker located on the generator itself. If it has tripped, switch it completely off before flipping it back on. If that doesn’t work, check the breakers in your home’s main electrical panel that are connected to the generator. If the breakers continue to trip, you may have an overload or a more serious electrical issue that requires a professional electrician to diagnose.

**What should I do if my generator starts making a strange noise?** Unusual noises like grinding, clanking, or loud popping are your generator’s way of telling you something is seriously wrong internally. Do not ignore these sounds. You should safely shut down the unit to prevent further, more expensive damage to the engine. This is not a DIY situation, so your next step should be to call a qualified technician to diagnose and resolve the problem.

OK great work. First, It sounds like you have a 2 psi gas system with regulators at each appliance to reduce it to 7"wc. Sometimes if the regulator doesn't react fast enough pressure in excess of 7"wc can end up on the service side of the demand regulator and it can't open against more then about 17"wc (1 psi=about 24"wc). This can be caused by several things including a clogged atmospheric vent on the gas regulator for the generator. A picture of that regulator and some info about how far away it is from the generator will help. Also they have recently come out with a stronger fuel solenoid which I have found eliminates this problem most of the time. 2) The butterfly should be spring loaded and freely move from closed to open and back. It appears that something is holding it open a little, that may be from the factory but I have never seen it. With the unit off take your finger and see if you can move it and see if it is free both ways. If possible closely examine that little bump to see if it looks like it is supposed to be there as a stop or to actually hold the butterfly open some. You may be able to watch it operate with a mirror while you try to start the unit in manual, be careful of the moving parts! You may have to take the mixer off as an assembly to get a better look at it. Good Luck, Peddler

OK do not take the bosch governor/throttle body apart, it is working. If when you jump 12VDC to the 14 wire on the fuel solenoid and you hear gas rushing through then the demand regulator has a problem. When the start sequence is initiated the fuel solenoid is opened as soon as the board confirms that the unit is turning over by the mag pick up. If the fuel solenoid is open gas will still not flow until the demand diaphragm is depressed by negitive pressure in the hose that goes up to the throttle body. That is what the half choke does help increase the negitive pressure by closing off the air intake. As the diaphragn moves in it pushes on a lever that opens an orfice that allows gas to flow through the hose to the throttle body. if there is no negative pressure from the engine turning over there should be no fuel flow. I recomend two things first verify that you only have 7"wc pressure at the inside (the generator) regulator. Then jump the 12VDC to the fuel solenoid and see if you can hear fuel flowing. If you do you will need to disassemble the demand regulator to see if the lever has come off or something else is not right. The picture I wanted you to send was one of the gas regulator in the line approaching the generator if there is one. That would reduce the pressure from a 2psi system to a 7"wc pressure. Good Luck, Peddler

It has a mag PU but you would get a flashing overspeed fault on the R-200 panel if the mag P/U wasn't sending its signal. I still think you may have a flooding problem. If the Solenoid can't open because high pressure gas (2 psi) has gotten past the second stage regulator before the generator then the suction pulls very hard on the demand diaphragm and can cause the lever to pop off and then flood and 90% of the time they won't start. Take a picture of the regulator that is on the line going to the generator before the enclosure so I can see what type you have and how it'd plumbed. Good Luck, Peddler

### Why Your Generator Won’t Start (and How to Fix It Fast)

- [September 22, 2025](https://ctgeneratorservice.com/2025/09/22/)
- 9:31 am

When your generator won’t start during an outage, the regulator could be the hidden culprit. Propane/natural gas regulator failure often causes startup and running problems. Discover why this happens, warning signs to look for, and how CT Generator Service keeps Connecticut homes powered when it matters most.

![](https://ctgeneratorservice.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Why-Your-Generator-Wont-Start-and-How-to-Fix-It-Fast-CT-Generator-Service-1024x538.jpg)

Imagine this: a storm rolls through Connecticut. The power’s out. You walk to your trusty generator, confident it will save the day. You hit the start button… and nothing. Or worse—it sputters, starts for a moment, and dies.

Nine times out of ten, homeowners blame the fuel supply or the generator itself. But here’s a hidden culprit most people don’t think about: **propane/natural gas regulator failure**.

At CT Generator Service, we see this problem all the time, and it’s often the reason a generator won’t start or stay running. Let’s break it down in simple terms and show you how to spot (and solve) this issue before it leaves you in the dark.

#### **What Is a Gas Regulator (and Why Should You Care)?**

Think of the regulator as the **traffic cop** of your generator’s fuel system.

- It controls the **pressure** of the propane or natural gas entering your generator.

- Too much pressure? The engine floods.

- Too little pressure? The engine starves.


In both cases, your generator won’t run reliably—or at all.

#### **Why Do Regulators Fail?**

Like any mechanical part, regulators wear down over time. Here are the **most common causes** we see:

- **Age & wear** – Springs, diaphragms, and seals inside the regulator break down.

- **Contamination** – Dirt, rust, or oil in the gas line clog the regulator.

- **Extreme weather** – Cold Connecticut winters cause condensation and freezing.

- **Improper installation** – A poorly sized or installed regulator can fail prematurely.


#### **Signs Your Regulator Is the Problem**

Wondering if a faulty regulator is behind your generator’s trouble? Watch for these symptoms:

- Generator cranks but **doesn’t start**
- Generator starts but **stalls after a few seconds**
- **Sputtering or backfiring** during operation

- **Uneven power output** (lights flicker, appliances struggle)


If you’ve already checked the battery and fuel supply, chances are high the regulator is the real culprit.

#### **What You Can Do (Before Calling a Pro)**

Here’s what you can safely try:

1. **Check your fuel source** – Make sure your propane tank has fuel or your natural gas line isn’t shut off.

2. **Inspect for visible damage** – Look for ice buildup, rust, or cracks around the regulator.

3. **Listen for leaks** – A hissing sound may indicate gas escaping. (⚠️ If you smell gas, turn everything off immediately and call a professional.)


👉 If the issue persists, it’s time to call in a certified technician. Regulators are **not DIY-friendly**—they deal with pressurized gas and require precise calibration.

@ GreggM: I have had two of the Generacs.

Yours is obviously connected to NG or appears to be.

So inside the chassis is a solenoid that opens into a chamber before the gas enters the carburetor.

My guess is that a bad pressure regulator will flood the chamber.

Example: When adding a NG/propane regulator to a gasoline generator you have an adjustment screw to regulate speed to set the hertz.

That is what you are doing when you close off the valve.

Too much NG/propane will flood the chamber in the generator as well as too little starves it.

You can read your installers manual and you can see that the inches of water setting on the regulator must be adhered too.

I would guess that your regulator has a ruptured diaphram.

I have see it before, and most of the time the generator will not run period.

A new regulator might be in order and start from there.

I did not comment on Youtube because they track your every move.

By the way, you have a earlier model and I too had one of these.

You just might as a precaution get you a spare Voltage regulator because it will surely let you down when you need it most.

Mine sent the 120 vac to 165 vac and blew a lot of items in the house.

I needed a rocker switch too as mine failed to lock.

Check with a dealer and get spares if you value your generator because it is one of the best ones to be made lately.

The new ones have too much electronic crap in them.

Example: Last week I had a orange led on that indicated trouble.

Instructions indicate a battery problem.

Actually it was just a battery check lite that gave me a start.

Foolish electronic junk added to get money out of owners.

I agree, I would check into the regulator as well.

Can you just crack the ball valve and try starting again. If you can manually manipulate the valve and keep the generator running that would pretty much confirm a regulator issue.

I’m not a 100%, but it’s my understanding that a torn diaphragm would cause no fueling, not flooding. The diaphragm is spring loaded in a normally closed position until engine vacuum pulls it open. This makes sense as a fail safe.

It’s possible that debris On the sealing surface might be causing the regulator to not seal and cause a flood condition.
